# Statistics essentials

- The mean is the average; the median is the middle value; the mode is the most frequent.
- The median resists outliers better than the mean in skewed data like incomes.
- Variance averages squared deviations; standard deviation is its square root in original units.
- The 68-95-99.7 rule: that share of normal data falls within 1, 2, 3 standard deviations.
- Correlation measures linear association (-1 to 1); correlation never implies causation alone.
- A p-value is the probability of data this extreme assuming the null hypothesis, not the probability the hypothesis is true.
- Statistical significance (p < 0.05 by convention) is not the same as practical importance.
- Confidence intervals bracket plausible parameter values; wider intervals mean less precision.
- The law of large numbers: sample averages converge to the expected value as samples grow.
- Selection bias and survivorship bias distort conclusions more often than faulty math does.
- Base rates matter: rare conditions produce mostly false positives even with accurate tests.
- Regression to the mean: extreme measurements tend to look more average on retest.
- Simpson's paradox: trends in subgroups can reverse when the groups are combined.
- Always plot data first; identical summary statistics can hide wildly different shapes (Anscombe).
- Sample size drives power: small samples cannot reliably detect small effects.
- Random sampling beats big biased samples; representativeness matters more than raw volume.
- A/B tests need pre-declared metrics, sufficient runtime, and randomization to be trustworthy.
- Percent change uses `(new - old) / old`; percentage points are the arithmetic difference of percents.
